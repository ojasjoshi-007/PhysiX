03

Physics Simulation Engine & Numerical Accuracy

PhysiX utilizes high-precision Newtonian rigid-body physics (Matter.js 2D), discrete integration algorithms, numerical kinematic solvers, and optoelectronic transfer equations. While simulations are calibrated against experimental textbook physics:

  • Discrete Time-Step Limits: High-velocity ballistic trajectories and collision events depend on discrete delta-time ($\Delta t$) stepping and the browser rendering frame rate. Minor integration drift or floating-point variations may occur under extreme boundary parameters.
  • Simplified Models: Certain real-world non-linear perturbations (such as atmospheric turbulence, humidity-dependent air resistance, or non-uniform laser speckle patterns) may be idealized for clarity of foundational physics concepts.
  • No Critical Infrastructure Use: PhysiX models are calibrated for educational and pedagogical training; they must not be used as the sole safety-critical calculations for physical aerospace, structural engineering, or life-critical designs.

Information We Collect

PhysiX adheres to a strict principle of data minimization. We only collect the bare minimum telemetry necessary to support student laboratory progression:

  • Account Credentials: If you create an account, we record your verified email address, display name, and avatar index through Google Firebase Authentication.
  • Mastery & XP Progression: Cumulative physics XP, unlocked achievements, daily login streaks, and quiz performance metrics.
  • Observation Records: Laboratory trial values (e.g. projectile launch angle $\theta$, initial velocity $v_0$, fiber distance $L$, optical ring diameter $W$, color sensor channel frequencies).
02

How Laboratory Telemetry is Utilized

Your simulation measurements and experiment notes are used exclusively to:

  • Calculate percentage error between theoretical formulas and virtual experimental runs.
  • Automatically generate printable PDF and CSV lab reports for classroom submissions.
  • Provide personalized feedback and contextual scientific hints through the Vectra AI assistant.
03

Local Browser Storage vs Cloud Synchronization

PhysiX provides a seamless dual-storage architecture:

  • Guest Mode: If you use PhysiX without signing in, all simulation settings, observation tables, and bookmarks are written solely to your device's localStorage. No telemetry leaves your machine.
  • Authenticated Mode: When signed in, your telemetry syncs via Google Firestore so you can access your experiments across classroom computers, tablets, and personal laptops.
04

Infrastructure & Security Practices

All network communications between the PhysiX client and backend services are encrypted in transit using industry-standard TLS 1.3 (HTTPS). Data stored in Google Firestore is protected by strict granular security rules ensuring that only authenticated users can read or write their own laboratory records.

05

Third-Party Sub-Processors

PhysiX engages a minimal set of enterprise-grade cloud sub-processors strictly for authentication and data storage:

  • Google Firebase: Authentication, user profile verification, and real-time Firestore database synchronization.
  • Cloudflare / GitHub Pages / Vite: Content delivery network and static asset serving.

We do not integrate any advertising SDKs, behavioral telemetry trackers, or social media tracking pixels.

  • Request complete, permanent deletion of your Firebase account and associated Firestore records by contacting support or clearing your student profile.

3 Laboratory Precautions & Practical Guidelines

3 Scientific Equations, Physical Model & Graph Interpretation

Total Decibel Optical Power Loss

$\text{Loss (dB)} = 10 \cdot \log_{10}\left( \frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{out}}} \right)$

Where $P_{\text{in}}$ is the optical power launched into the fiber and $P_{\text{out}}$ is the optical power received at the detector (both expressed in matching milliwatt units).

Distributed Attenuation Coefficient (α)

$\alpha = \left( \frac{1000}{L} \right) \cdot 10 \cdot \log_{10}\left( \frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{out}}} \right) \quad [\text{dB/km}]$

$\alpha = \left( \frac{10}{L} \right) \cdot \log_{10}\left( \frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{out}}} \right) \quad [\text{dB/m}]$

Where $L$ is the transmission fiber length in metres. For standard commercial PMMA plastic optical fiber at $\lambda = 650\text{ nm}$, $\alpha \approx 180\text{ dB/km} = 0.180\text{ dB/m}$.

Transmitted Optical Power (Bouguer-Lambert-Beer Law)

$P_{\text{out}} = P_{\text{in coupled}} \cdot 10^{-\frac{A_{\text{total}}}{10}}$

Where total loss $A_{\text{total}} = \left(\frac{\alpha}{1000} \cdot L\right) + A_{\text{coupling}} + A_{\text{bend}}$:
• Transverse Misalignment Loss: $\eta = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta x^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ with insertion loss $A_{\text{coupling}} = -10\log_{10}(\eta)$.
• Macrobending Radiation Leakage: $0.0\text{ dB}$ (Straight), $0.8\text{ dB}$ (Moderate bend), and $2.5\text{ dB}$ (Sharp bend).

Aim: To determine the Numerical Aperture (NA) and Acceptance Angle (θ_a) of an optical fibre using the output light spot divergence method on a graduated optical bench.

Apparatus: Optical fibre trainer kit, power supply, 650nm red laser diode, step-index optical fibre cable, precision NA measurement jig, white screen with concentric circles (1.0cm to 3.5cm), graduated bench scale.

Theoretical Foundations

  • Optical Fibre: A cylindrical dielectric waveguide composed of a high-index Core (n₁) surrounded by a lower-index Cladding (n₂), where n₁ > n₂.
  • Total Internal Reflection (TIR): Light rays incident at angles greater than the critical angle θ_c = arcsin(n₂/n₁) undergo total reflection at the core-cladding boundary.
  • Acceptance Cone: The maximum cone of light rays incident on the fiber core face that will be guided through the fiber by TIR.
  • Diverging Output Spot: Upon exiting the fiber tip, the light beam diverges conically with half-angle θ_a, forming a circular spot of diameter W on a screen at distance L.

Aim & Apparatus

Aim: To investigate the Hall Effect in semiconductor and metal probes, measure the transverse Hall voltage ($V_H$) as a function of magnetic flux density ($B$) and constant current ($I$), identify charge carrier type ($n\text{-type}$ vs $p\text{-type}$), and calculate carrier concentration ($n$) and Hall mobility ($\mu_H$).

Apparatus: Electromagnet with soft-iron pole pieces (0 to 0.8 T), regulated constant current DC power supply (0 to 50 mA), electromagnet energizing power supply (0 to 5 A), calibrated Hall probe fixtures ($n\text{-Ge}$, $p\text{-Ge}$, $\text{InAs}$, and $\text{Cu}$), digital Hall Gaussmeter, digital DC microvoltmeter (nV to mV), zero-null bridge potentiometer, 4-quadrant polarity reversal commutator.

Theoretical Principle & Lorentz Force

When a current-carrying conductor or semiconductor is placed in a transverse magnetic field $\vec{B}$, mobile charge carriers with drift velocity $\vec{v}_d$ experience a magnetic Lorentz deflection force:

$\vec{F}_L = q(\vec{v}_d \times \vec{B})$

This force deflects mobile charges laterally toward one side of the specimen, creating a charge separation. This accumulation establishes a transverse electrostatic electric field $\vec{E}_H$ (Hall field). In steady-state equilibrium, the transverse electric force balances the magnetic Lorentz force:
$q \cdot E_H = q \cdot v_d \cdot B \implies E_H = v_d \cdot B$

Hall Voltage Formula: V_H = (R_H · I · B) / t
Hall Coefficient: R_H = (V_H · t) / (I · B) = 1 / (n · q)
Carrier Concentration (Density): n = 1 / (|R_H| · e)
Hall Carrier Mobility: μ_H = |R_H| · σ = |R_H| / ρ
Hall Angle: tan(θ_H) = E_H / E_x = μ_H · B
Carrier Drift Velocity: v_d = I / (n · e · w · t) = J / (n · e)
Zero-Offset Cancellation: V_H = [(V₁ - V₂) + (V₃ - V₄)] / 4
Electrical Conductivity: σ = n · e · μ = 1 / ρ

Carrier Type Identification (n-type vs p-type)

  • n-type Semiconductor ($R_H < 0$): Conduction is dominated by negative electrons ($q = -e$). Electrons accumulate at the lateral edge, creating a negative potential with respect to the opposite edge. Observed in $n\text{-Ge}$ and $\text{InAs}$ crystals.
  • p-type Semiconductor ($R_H > 0$): Conduction is dominated by positive holes ($q = +e$). Holes accumulate at the same lateral edge, producing a positive Hall voltage. Observed in $p\text{-Ge}$ crystals.
  • Semiconductor vs Metal Contrast: Metals (like Copper) have extremely high carrier concentration ($n \sim 10^{28}\text{ m}^{-3}$), producing minute microvolt signals ($V_H \sim \mu\text{V}$). Semiconductors have significantly lower carrier concentrations ($n \sim 10^{20} - 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3}$), yielding strong millivolt signals ($V_H \sim \text{mV}$).

Step-by-Step Experimental Procedure

  1. Mount the Hall probe perpendicular between the soft-iron pole pieces of the electromagnet.
  2. With zero magnetic field ($B = 0\text{ T}$), pass a constant current $I$ (e.g., $5\text{ mA}$) through the probe and balance the zero-offset potentiometer to null misalignment voltage.
  3. Energize the electromagnet to set a calibrated magnetic flux density $B$ (0.1 to 0.8 T) monitored on the digital Gaussmeter.
  4. Record $V_1$ (forward $I$, forward $B$). Reverse field polarity to obtain $V_2$ (forward $I$, reverse $B$).
  5. Reverse current polarity to obtain $V_3$ (reverse $I$, forward $B$) and $V_4$ (reverse $I$, reverse $B$). Compute true Hall voltage: $V_H = \frac{(V_1 - V_2) + (V_3 - V_4)}{4}$.
  6. Plot $V_H$ vs $B$ at constant $I$ (slope gives $m_B = \frac{R_H I}{t}$) and $V_H$ vs $I$ at constant $B$ (slope gives $m_I = \frac{R_H B}{t}$).
  7. Calculate Hall coefficient $R_H$, carrier concentration $n = \frac{1}{|R_H| e}$, and mobility $\mu_H = |R_H| \sigma$.

Apparatus: Tunable diode laser source ($\lambda = 650\text{ nm}$ Red, $532\text{ nm}$ Green, $405\text{ nm}$ Violet), calibrated transmission diffraction grating (300, 600, 1200 lines/mm), precision optical bench rail (0.50 to 2.00 m), projection screen with millimeter scale, photoelectric detector.

Diffraction & Interference Principle

A diffraction grating consists of an array of $N$ parallel, equally spaced transparent slits of width $a$ separated by opaque intervals $b$. The grating element is $d = a + b = 1/N$. Under normal incidence, wavelets diffracted from adjacent slits have optical path difference $\Delta x = d \sin\theta$. Constructive multi-beam interference yields principal maxima when:

$d \cdot \sin\theta = m \cdot \lambda \quad (m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots)$

Grating Equation (Normal Incidence): d · sin(θ) = m · λ
Grating Pitch (Slit Spacing): d = (a + b) = 1 / N = 10⁻³ / (lines/mm) m
Fringe Angular Displacement: tan(θ) = y / L ⟹ sin(θ) = y / √(L² + y²)
Wavelength Determination: λ = (d · sin θ_m) / m = (d · y_m) / (m · √(L² + y_m²))
Angular Dispersion: D = dθ / dλ = m / (d · cos θ)
Chromatic Resolving Power: R = λ / Δλ = m · N_total
Maximum Observable Order: m_max = ⌊d / λ⌋ (since sin θ ≤ 1)
Absent Spectra (Missing Orders): d / a = m / p ⟹ (a + b) / a = m / p

Governing Wave Optics Principles

  • Fraunhofer Far-Field Diffraction: Incident planar wavefronts diffracted by $N$ parallel equidistant slits undergo coherent multi-beam interference, producing narrow, high-intensity principal maxima.
  • Order of Spectrum ($m$): The central spot ($m=0$) corresponds to undiffracted light ($\theta = 0^\circ$). First-order maxima ($m=\pm 1$) and second-order maxima ($m=\pm 2$) appear symmetrically on either side.
  • Chromatic Dispersion: Because $\sin\theta \propto \lambda$, longer wavelengths (Red $\sim 650\text{ nm}$) diffract at larger angles than shorter wavelengths (Violet $\sim 405\text{ nm}$), reversing the dispersion sequence of a prism.
  • Missing Orders (Extinction): If a principal maximum condition ($d\sin\theta = m\lambda$) coincides with a single-slit diffraction minimum ($a\sin\theta = p\lambda$), the overall diffraction envelope vanishes and that order is absent.

Step-by-Step Experimental Procedure

  1. Set up the optical bench rail and direct the diode laser beam perpendicular to the diffraction grating face ($\theta_i = 0^\circ$).
  2. Position the projection screen at a fixed distance $L$ (e.g., $1.00\text{ m}$) from the grating.
  3. Mark the central bright spot ($m = 0$) on the millimeter grid scale.
  4. Measure the lateral distances $y_{+1}$ and $y_{-1}$ to the first-order spots on either side. Calculate average displacement $y_1 = \frac{y_{+1} + y_{-1}}{2}$.
  5. Calculate the diffraction angle $\sin\theta_1 = \frac{y_1}{\sqrt{L^2 + y_1^2}}$ and compute laser wavelength $\lambda = d \sin\theta_1$.
  6. Repeat the measurement for the second order ($m = 2$) and for different grating elements ($N = 300, 600, 1200\text{ lines/mm}$).

Newtonian Rigid-Body Mechanics Principles

Every rigid body in the simulation obeys Newton's laws of motion. When external forces or collision impulses act upon a body of mass $m$, its linear momentum $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$ changes according to the impulse-momentum theorem:

$\vec{J} = \int \vec{F}_{\text{net}} \, dt = \Delta\vec{p} = m(\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i)$

Newton's Second Law: F_net = m · a = dp / dt
Impulse-Momentum Theorem: J = ∫ F dt = Δp = m(v_f - v_i)
Conservation of Linear Momentum: ∑ m_i · v_i = ∑ m_i · v_f
Coefficient of Restitution: e = -(v₂f - v₁f) / (v₂i - v₁i)
Kinetic Energy: KE = (1/2)m v² = p² / (2m)

Aim & Semiconductor Physics

Aim: To study the voltage-current ($V-I$) characteristics of a P-N junction diode under forward and reverse bias conditions, determine the knee voltage ($V_\gamma$), and calculate dynamic forward and reverse resistances.

Theory: Under forward bias ($V > 0$), the external electric field opposes the built-in barrier potential, reducing the depletion width. Once the applied voltage exceeds the cut-in knee threshold ($V_\gamma \approx 0.65\text{ V}$ for silicon), majority charge carriers diffuse across the junction, resulting in exponential current growth according to the Shockley diode equation:

$I = I_s \left( e^{\frac{q V}{\eta k_B T}} - 1 \right)$

Governing Formulas

Shockley Diode Equation: I = I_s · [exp(qV / ηk_B T) - 1]
Thermal Voltage (at 300 K): V_T = k_B T / q ≈ 25.85 mV
Dynamic Forward Resistance: r_f = ΔV_f / ΔI_f = (η V_T) / I_f
Dynamic Reverse Resistance: r_r = ΔV_r / ΔI_r
Reverse Saturation Current: I ≈ -I_s (for V_r >> V_T)

Aim & Optical Waveguide Theory

Aim: To measure optical power attenuation in a step-index plastic optical fiber (POF) as a function of propagation length, calculate total attenuation in decibels ($A_{\text{dB}}$), and determine the attenuation coefficient ($\alpha$ in $\text{dB/km}$).

Theory: When monochromatic light ($\lambda = 650\text{ nm}$) propagates along an optical fiber waveguide, optical power decreases exponentially with length $L$ in accordance with the Bouguer-Lambert-Beer law:

$P_{\text{out}} = P_{\text{in}} \times 10^{-\frac{\alpha L}{10}}$
Losses originate from intrinsic material attenuation (Rayleigh scattering from sub-microscopic polymer fluctuations and carbon-hydrogen vibrational overtone absorption) and extrinsic factors (Fresnel reflections, facet misalignment, and macrobending radiation leakage).

Governing Attenuation Formulas

Total Attenuation (dB): A_dB = 10 · log10(P_in / P_out)
Attenuation Coefficient per km: α = (1000 / L_m) · 10 · log10(P_in / P_out)
Exponential Power Decay: P_out = P_in · 10^(-α · L / 10)
Decibel-Milliwatt Conversion: P_dBm = 10 · log10(P_mW / 1 mW)
